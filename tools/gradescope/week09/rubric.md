# Week 9 — hand-marked question (25 points)

**Is CO2 rising faster than it used to?**

The autograder scores the two code parts. This is the part it cannot: mark it in Gradescope as a manual rubric item worth 25, on top of the autograder's score.

## What the student was asked

### ✏️ Your turn 8

Two or three sentences in the cell below, and every claim in them has to be one of *your* numbers.

Quote at least three: the year you chose to quote in part 2, the spread between your four
crossing years, and the held-out miss from your turn 3 of the degree you chose. Then answer this:
a newspaper wants one year. What do you give them, and what does the spread tell their reader
about how much of that year came from the record and how much from your choice of curve?

## Criteria

| Points | A full-credit answer does this |
|---:|---|
| 6 | Quotes at least three of their own numbers: the crossing year they chose in part 2, the spread between the four crossing years (48 years, 2051 to 2099), and the turn 3 held-out miss of the degree behind their chosen year, 1.57 ppm for degree 2 or 14.831 ppm for degree 1. |
| 6 | Commits to a single year for the newspaper and justifies it by held-out performance rather than preference, contrasting degree 2's 1.57 ppm miss with degree 1's 14.831 ppm and noting that a straight line's date assumes the rise stops accelerating the day the record ends. A defended degree-1 or training-window answer earns this if it argues from the held-out numbers. |
| 6 | Separates the two sources of the spread with their own four years, saying which moves the answer further, the degree or the training window, and by how many years. Asserting that one matters more without the year counts does not earn this item. |
| 7 | Tells the reader the honest thing: the gap between the earliest and the latest crossing year is not a measurement of anything, it is the curve the student chose to draw through the same points, so the defensible claim is a decade-scale range rather than one year carrying false precision. |

## The model answer

Not a template to match word for word — a student who reaches the same conclusions from their own numbers, in their own words, has answered it.

I would give them 2051, from the parabola fitted to the whole record. My four
crossing years were 2075 and 2051 on the whole record and
2099 and 2053 on 1959–2000, a spread of
48 years. The degree matters more than the training window does: changing the
degree moved my answer by 24 years on the whole record and
46 years on the 1959–2000 fit,
while changing the
window moved it 24 years for degree 1 and only
2 for degree 2. Degree 1 is the one I
distrust, because on the held-out years it missed by 14.831 ppm against
1.57 ppm for degree 2, and its 500 ppm date assumes the rise stops
accelerating on the day the record ends. So the honest thing to tell the reader is that the data
says "some time this century, most likely mid-century", and that the difference between
2051 and 2099 is not a measurement at
all — it is which curve I chose to draw through the same points.

## The week's takeaways, for context

1. Too simple misses the pattern; too flexible memorises the noise.
2. Hold out data you already have, then check — it is the cheapest honest test there is.
3. The CO2 curve is bending: a straight line fitted to the whole record starts ten ppm below the record's own first year.
