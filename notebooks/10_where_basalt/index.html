    EPS 88 · PyEarth. Open your own copy on DataHub: click here.

    Basalt is the commonest volcanic rock on Earth: the crust under every ocean is made of it. It is made in three different places, where the mantle melts, and a geologist often holds a piece without knowing which. California's Coast Ranges, for example, hold slices of old sea floor lifted onto the continent, and whether that crust was made at a mid-ocean ridge or above a sinking plate decides what story the rocks tell. The rock cannot say where it came from. Its chemistry can.

    1. What in a basalt's chemistry records where it was made?¶

    A rock with no address. California's Coast Ranges hold slices of old sea floor, scraped off a sinking plate and lifted onto the continent. Was that crust made at a mid-ocean ridge, above a sinking plate, or on an ocean island? The answer decides the history the rocks tell, and the rock itself cannot say. Its chemistry can.

    Basalt has three main homes in the ocean. Basalt is made in three places, where the mantle melts: where two plates pull apart (a mid-ocean ridge), where one plate sinks under another (an island arc), and over a hot plume, usually far from any plate edge (an ocean island). (It also forms on the continents, where they rift apart or flood with lava; today's rocks come from the three ocean homes.) Geologists call the kind of place a rock was made its tectonic setting. In the data it is the column setting, with three values named after the rock: IAB (island-arc basalt), MORB (mid-ocean-ridge basalt) and OIB (ocean-island basalt).

    Among its features: an island arc above a sinking plate, a shield volcano over a hot spot, and a mid-ocean ridge where two plates pull apart. Cross-section by José F. Vigil, USGS (public domain), via Wikimedia Commons.

    From the outside the three look alike: dark fine-grained rock, often in rounded "pillows" where lava met cold sea water:

    Pillow basalt on the sea floor. Photo: NOAA (public domain), via Wikimedia Commons.

    So geologists measure what is inside. A rock analysis gives the common elements as oxides, in percent of the rock's weight (SiO2, TiO2), and the rare ones in parts per million (V, Sr).

    2. How well does last week's model sort the rocks?¶

    Last week's logistic regression, in last week's symbols. For each event it worked out one number, $z$, from the two magnitudes, and bent $z$ into a probability with the S-curve:

    $$p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad z = a \times \text{mb} + b \times \text{Ms} + c$$

    $p$ is the probability that the event is an explosion, and fit chose the three numbers $a$, $b$ and $c$. Where $z = 0$, $p = 0.5$: those events lie on a straight line, the decision boundary, with explosions predicted on one side and earthquakes on the other.

    With three settings, three z's. For a rock with titanium Ti and potassium K (both in weight percent), the model works out one $z$ for each setting:

    $$z_{\text{IAB}} = a_1 \times \text{Ti} + b_1 \times \text{K} + c_1, \quad z_{\text{MORB}} = a_2 \times \text{Ti} + b_2 \times \text{K} + c_2, \quad z_{\text{OIB}} = a_3 \times \text{Ti} + b_3 \times \text{K} + c_3$$

    It turns the three into three probabilities that add up to 1, and predicts the setting with the largest. Where two settings' $z$'s are equal, a straight line runs between them, so the map is cut into three regions with straight edges. fit now chooses nine numbers instead of three.

    def draw_regions(model, title):
    """Colour a TiO2-K2O map by what a fitted model predicts at each point, and draw the training rocks on it."""
    # 1. a grid of made-up rocks, every 0.05 percent of TiO2 and of K2O
    ti_grid, k_grid = [], []
    for ti in np.arange(0, 4.51, 0.05):
        for k in np.arange(0, 3.01, 0.05):
            ti_grid.append(ti)
            k_grid.append(k)
    grid = pd.DataFrame({"TiO2_wt_percent": ti_grid, "K2O_wt_percent": k_grid})
    # 2. the model's prediction for each made-up rock
    predicted = model.predict(grid)
    # 3. colour each made-up rock by its prediction, and draw the real training rocks on top
    for setting, colour, light in [("IAB", "tab:blue", "#c6dbef"), ("MORB", "tab:orange", "#fdd0a2"), ("OIB", "tab:green", "#c7e9c0")]:

    The library does all five rounds in one line:

    scores = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=5)

    model is a new, unfitted model; X the columns and y the labels of the training rocks; cv=5 asks for five parts. The library cuts the parts itself, keeping each setting's share the same in every part, then fits and scores five times and returns the five scores, one per round.

    5. Can a support vector machine beat them?¶

    The widest gap. Of all the lines separating the two groups, take the one leaving the widest gap. Many lines can part two groups of rocks. The SVM picks the one that leaves the widest empty band between them, so a new rock has the most room to land on its own side. The rocks on the edge of the band, the support vectors, alone fix the line, which gives the method its name.

    Two groups (blue, green) and the line that parts them (red) with the widest gap (yellow band); the circled points on the edge of the band are the support vectors. Figure: Larhmam (CC BY-SA 4.0), via Wikimedia Commons.

    Real rocks overlap, so no band is empty: the SVM lets a few rocks sit inside the band, or even on the wrong side, in exchange for a wider gap. With three settings, SVC finds a widest gap for each pair (arc–ridge, arc–island, ridge–island), and the pairs vote. And plain SVC() may bend its edges into curves to follow the clouds of rocks; the idea, the widest gap, is the same.

    A gap is a distance, and a distance adds up the differences in each column in that column's own units. The oxides' standard deviations differ a lot:

    Scaling fixes this: from each column subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation,

    $$x_\text{scaled} = \frac{x - \text{mean}}{\text{std}}$$

    so every column has mean 0 and spread 1. StandardScaler does it in two steps, like a model: .fit(table) learns each column's mean and standard deviation, and .transform(table) applies them. A simple case: 2 and 6 have mean 4 and standard deviation 2, so they become −1 and 1.

    Week 10 summary¶

    The science¶

    Where was this basalt made?
    A basalt's chemistry records where it formed. Its big oxides (silica about 50 percent, Al2O3, CaO, MgO, Na2O) build its minerals and barely differ between settings; three small ones differ a lot: P2O5 2.9 times, TiO2 3.1 times and K2O 5.9 times from the lowest setting median to the highest. They fit badly in mantle minerals and gather in the melt, so how much the mantle melts, and whether water from a sinking plate is present, sets how much of them a basalt holds. A training score is not the model's score. Training, validation and test sets each have one job: the training rocks fit, the validation rocks compare, and the test rocks, touched by no decision, check once whether the comparison holds. A forest scores 1.000 on the rocks it learned from and less on rocks held out. Cross-validation chooses the model: five parts, each held out once, give a fair score and its spread (cross_val_score, cv=5). One validation split moved the same model's score from 0.867 to 0.967. Three classifiers, compared. Logistic regression, the random forest and the SVM share fit, predict and score, so trying another is one line. Logistic regression draws straight edges: simple and readable, the model to beat, but it cannot bend. A random forest bends to the data and takes columns in any units, but cannot be read as one rule. An SVM draws smooth curves and needs scaled columns. On nine oxides the flexible models gain a little (0.931 each against 0.902); on titanium and potassium they gain little or nothing (forest 0.862, scaled SVM 0.877, against 0.868 for the lines). The method is chosen by cross-validation, not by reputation.

    • Tectonic settings of basalt. Basalt is made in three places, where the mantle melts: where two plates pull apart (a mid-ocean ridge), where one plate sinks under another (an island arc), and over a hot plume, usually far from any plate edge (an ocean island).
    • Major oxide, trace element. A rock analysis gives the common elements as oxides, in percent of the rock's weight (SiO2, TiO2), and the rare ones in parts per million (V, Sr).
    • Feature importance. How much of a forest's decisions each column carried — the model's own account of what it used.
    • Cross-validation. Cut the rows into five parts; hold out each part in turn, train on the other four, and score on the one held out. Five scores, and every row tested once.
    • Decision tree. A flowchart of yes/no questions, learned from the data instead of written by hand.
    • Random forest. Ask many slightly different trees and take a vote.
    • SVM. Of all the lines separating the two groups, take the one leaving the widest gap.

    Lines drawn by hand: Shervais's diagram. In 1982 the geologist John Shervais plotted titanium against vanadium. Vanadium alone barely differs between the settings (median 280, 271 and 274 ppm in the arc, ridge and island training rocks). Shervais used it as a ruler. As a melt cools and grows crystals, vanadium and titanium change together, so their ratio, Ti/V, changes less than either and keeps the mark of the melting. And an arc's melt is wet and oxidised, which lets more vanadium into it, so arc basalt holds much vanadium for its titanium. He drew two lines, at Ti/V = 20 and Ti/V = 50: arc rocks below 20, ridge rocks between, ocean islands above 50. (That last field is for the alkaline ocean-island basalts; island tholeiites, like most of Hawaii's lava, plot between 20 and 50 with the ridges.)
