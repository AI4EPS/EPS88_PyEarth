    How old is the universe?¶

    EPS 88 · PyEarth. Open your own copy on DataHub: click here.

    In 1929 nobody knew whether the universe was standing still, shrinking or growing. The astronomer Edwin Hubble compared two measurements for nearby galaxies: how far away each one is, and how fast it is moving away from us. Plotted against each other, the points fell near a straight line: the farther a galaxy, the faster it leaves. That line means the universe is expanding, and its slope says how fast. Run the expansion backwards and every galaxy was once in the same place; the slope tells you how long ago.

    Today you repeat Hubble's measurement with much better data: thirty-six exploding stars from a study led by the astronomer Wendy Freedman. You will find the best straight line through them three ways, by guessing, by trying every line and by a formula, then in one line of scikit-learn; check how good the line is; and turn its slope into an age for the universe. At home you try the same method on two more datasets, and find out where a straight line stops working.

    1. How do we know the universe is expanding?¶

    To test whether the universe is expanding you need two measurements for each galaxy: how fast it is moving away from us, and how far away it is.

    Speed comes from colour. You have heard the pitch of a siren drop as a fire engine passes you (listen to one). Sound is a wave, and the length of one wave, from crest to crest, is its wavelength. When the source moves away from you, each new crest starts from a little farther away, so the crests reach you farther apart: the wave arrives stretched. A longer sound wave has a lower pitch (the figure says frequency: fewer waves a second). This is the Doppler effect. In the picture, the car is moving to the right: the person it drives toward gets short waves and a high pitch, the person it leaves behind gets long waves and a low pitch.

    Figure from The Physics Classroom.

    Light is a wave too: when a galaxy moves away from us its light arrives with a longer wavelength, and a longer light wave is a redder colour. Astronomers call that a redshift.

    How do we know how long the waves were when they left? Hydrogen absorbs light at a few exact wavelengths (measured in nanometres, billionths of a metre), and they show as dark lines in the light of a star: the same pattern everywhere in the universe, like a barcode. In the light of a galaxy that is moving away, the whole barcode sits at longer wavelengths, toward the red end of the rainbow; in the light of one moving toward us, toward the blue:

    Figures from A-level Physics Tutor.

    The size of the shift gives the speed: for speeds well below light's, each wavelength grows by the fraction (the galaxy's speed) / (the speed of light). The fastest galaxy in today's table is leaving at 30,253 km/s, about a tenth of the speed of light, so every one of its lines sits a tenth farther along: hydrogen's red line, at 656 nanometres in a laboratory, arrives at 722. The far-red lines move more than the violet ones, because each one moves by the same fraction of itself: the barcode is stretched, not slid.

    Distance comes from brightness. A lamp looks dimmer the farther away it is. So if you know how bright a lamp really is, how bright it looks tells you its distance. Astronomers call such a lamp a standard candle, and a Type Ia supernova is one. It is the explosion of a white dwarf, the small, dense remains of a star, after it has gained matter from a companion star or merged with another white dwarf. These explosions are so alike that astronomers can work out how bright each one really was at its peak. They are also bright enough to be seen in galaxies more than a billion light-years away. Distances that large are measured in megaparsecs (Mpc). One megaparsec is 3.26 million light-years: light, the fastest thing there is, takes 3.26 million years to cross it.

    Each row of supernovae is one supernova. VCMB is the speed of its galaxy away from us, in km/s, with our own motion taken out: the CMB in the name is the cosmic microwave background, the oldest light in the universe, which astronomers use as the fixed background to measure speeds against. D(Mpc) is its distance in megaparsecs. That column name has brackets in it, so it always goes in quotes: supernovae["D(Mpc)"].

    Farther means faster, and the points fall close to a straight line. Every distant galaxy is moving away from us, and a galaxy twice as far leaves about twice as fast. That is what an expanding universe looks like from inside it. Think of dots drawn on a rubber band, one centimetre apart, and stretch the band to one and a half times its length in one second. Seen from your dot, the dot that was 1 cm away is now at 1.5 cm: it moved 0.5 cm. The dot that was 4 cm away is now at 6 cm: it moved 2 cm, in the same second, so four times as far means four times as fast. Stand on any other dot and you see the same thing. No dot is the centre; the band is simply stretching everywhere.

    How fast the universe is expanding is the slope of that line: how many km/s of speed each megaparsec of distance adds. So the measurement you need is the slope of the best straight line through these dots, and section 2 teaches how to find it.

    2. How do we find the best straight line?¶

    A straight line is two numbers. Write $y$ for the thing up the page and $x$ for the thing across. Then every straight line is

    $$y = a \, x + b$$

    $a$ is the slope: how much $y$ grows for each unit of $x$. $b$ is the intercept: the value of $y$ where $x$ is zero, where the line crosses the vertical axis. Choose $a$ and $b$ and you have chosen the line. Finding the best line through a cloud of points means choosing the best pair. This section finds it for the supernovae three ways, each better than the last: by guessing, by trying every pair, and by a formula. Then scikit-learn does it in one line.

    Judging by eye is not enough. Two guesses that both look close cannot be told apart by looking. To choose between them you need a number that says how badly a line misses its points.

    A residual is the real value minus the line's value.

    $$\text{residual} = \text{real value} - \text{the line's value}$$

    Take the first supernova in the table, SN1990O, 134.7 Mpc away at 9,065 km/s, and a reasonable guess, $a = 72$ and $b = 300$. The line gives it $72 \times 134.7 + 300 = 9,998$ km/s. Its residual for that line is $9,065 - 9,998 = -933$ km/s: the point sits that far below the line. A point above the line has a positive residual.

    To judge a whole line with one number, use the root mean squared error, RMSE. A line has thirty-six residuals. Square each one, so that misses above and below the line cannot cancel; take the mean of the squares; take the square root, which brings the number back to km/s:

    $$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\text{mean of the squared residuals}}$$

    The RMSE is the typical miss of the line, in the units of the data. Try it on three residuals, 2, −2 and 1. Added as they are they give 1, as if the line hardly missed. Their squares are 4, 4 and 1, the mean is 3, and the square root of 3 is 1.7. In Python the square root is a power of one half: (mean) ** 0.5. Squaring makes a line care most about its worst misses; the number a fit tries to make small is also called its loss. The next cell scores your four guesses, and the example line.

Finding it this way is called least squares: the line with the smallest RMSE is the line with the smallest total of squared residuals, the same thing.

    Trying every pair works, but it is slow and it only finds pairs on the grid. For a straight line there is a formula that goes to the bottom of the valley directly.

    The formula. Call the distances $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ and the speeds $y_1, \ldots, y_n$, with $n = 36$, and let $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ be their averages. The $a$ and $b$ with the smallest RMSE are

    $$a = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}, \qquad b = \bar{y} - a\,\bar{x}$$

    The top of $a$ adds up, over the points, how far each one is from the average in $x$ times how far it is in $y$: positive when the two go together. The bottom is how spread out the $x$ are. And $b$ makes the line pass through the point of averages, $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$. Proving that this pair is the bottom of the valley takes calculus; the next cell checks it instead.

    4. How old does that make the universe?¶

    This section uses slope from Your turn 5; run that cell again if you restarted.

    A friend arrives at your door and says: "I drove 120 km at 60 km/h." You know when your friend left: 120 divided by 60, two hours ago. Time is distance divided by speed. The same works for a galaxy. If it has always moved at the speed it has today, the time since it was right here is

    $$t = \frac{d}{v}$$

    Hubble's law says $v = H_0 d$. Put that in, and the distance cancels:

    $$t = \frac{d}{H_0 \, d} = \frac{1}{H_0}$$

    The same time for every galaxy, near or far: they all set out together. So one over the slope is an age for the universe.

    Step 1: a megaparsec is $3.0857 \times 10^{19}$ km. Multiply by that, and the Mpc cancels the Mpc while the km cancels the km, leaving seconds:

    Step 2: a year has $60 \times 60 \times 24 \times 365.25 = 31,557,600$ seconds. Divide by that, and the seconds cancel, leaving years:

    $$\frac{4.57 \times 10^{17}\ \text{s}}{31,557,600\ \text{s/year}} = 1.45 \times 10^{10}\ \text{years}$$

    Step 3: divide by a billion, $10^9$, to get 14.48 billion years.

    How to read the figure. In 2001 every bar was wide and they all overlapped, so the methods agreed. By 2016 the bars had shrunk, and the blue and the black no longer overlap: two careful methods give values 7 percent apart, more than their uncertainties allow. That disagreement is the Hubble tension. Either one method has an error nobody has found, or the model of the expansion that the CMB value relies on is missing something. It is one of the open questions of astronomy today, and Freedman explains it in this short video.

    Why the published age is 13.8 billion years and not 1/H₀. Your calculation assumed that every galaxy has always moved at the speed it has today. That is the friend who drove 120 km at a steady 60 km/h. If the friend drove slowly at first and fast at the end, "two hours ago" is only an estimate.

    The universe did both. For the first several billion years gravity slowed the expansion down. Since then it has been speeding up again, pushed by something astronomers call dark energy and do not yet understand. The published age comes from a model of that whole history, not from today's speed alone. The slowing and the speeding-up nearly cancel, which is why 1/H₀ lands within about 5 percent of 13.8: the right size, but not the exact age.

    The science¶

    How old is the universe?
    A slope can be an age: the slope of speed against distance is how fast the universe expands, and one over it is a time. The best straight line has the smallest total of squared residuals. The physical model can also fix a point on it: Hubble's law has no intercept, so the line is fitted through the origin. Before trusting a line, draw it on its data and look at what it misses: R² is one number, and the residual plot shows whether there is a pattern.

    • Residual. A residual is the real value minus the line's value.
    • Linear regression. Draw the best straight line. Best means the smallest root mean squared error: the line that misses its points least.
    • Fit through the origin. When the physical model fixes a point on the line, the fit keeps it.

    A curve is one more column. The residuals of Your turn 10 arch, so the far supernovae want a curve, not a line. A line is $y = a\,x + b$: one column, $x$, with one slope. Give the fit a second column and it finds a second slope for it:

    $$y = a_1\,x + a_2\,x_2 + b$$

    Now make that second column the first one squared, $x_2 = x^2$. The rule becomes $y = a_1\,x + a_2\,x^2 + b$, and that is a curve: it bends upward if $a_2$ is positive and bends over if $a_2$ is negative. To scikit-learn nothing has changed. It is still choosing numbers to make the RMSE as small as it can, by the same least squares, and it does not care that one column was made from another. The fit is the same call with two columns inside the double brackets, [["D(Mpc)", "d2"]]; coef_ then holds two numbers, $a_1$ and $a_2$, one per column, and intercept_ is still $b$.

    So the only new line is the one that makes the squared column. A table gets a new column when you assign to a name it does not have yet, with a whole old column on the right, as the earthquake week made region out of place:

    table["new"] = table["old"] ** 2

    Here the table is far_supernovae, the old column is D(Mpc) and the new one is d2.
