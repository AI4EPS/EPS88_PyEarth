    Suppose the carbon dioxide in the air jumped overnight to four times what it was before industry, and stayed there. How warm would the Earth get, and how fast? Nobody can run that experiment on the real planet, so climate scientists run it in their climate models.

    Today you get the first 20 years of one such run and predict the next 130. A neural network fitted to the 20 years fails after them. A network that must also obey the law of energy balance finds numbers for that law, which is hard to do by hand, and follows the 130 years it never saw. Then we check why it got them right, and find that its law is too simple. The point is the idea of a physics-informed network, on a problem where every answer can be checked.

    1. What happens when CO2 jumps to four times?¶

    We cannot quadruple the real Earth's CO2 to see what happens, so this experiment is run inside climate models. Climate model. A computer program that steps the physics of the air, the ocean, ice and land forward in time, on a grid around the globe; its output is a simulation, not a measurement. In the run called abrupt-4xCO2, a model starts from the climate before industry, and at year 0 its CO2 is multiplied by four and held there for 150 years. Nobody expects CO2 to jump like this; it is a test, one simple push whose response is easy to see.

    Watch: A Short Introduction to Climate Models: CMIP and CMIP6, a short video from the World Climate Research Programme, on what a climate model is and how modelling centres run the same experiments to compare their models.

    Fast at first, then slower. CESM2's Earth warms 0.87 °C in the first year and 3.96 °C by year 10, but only 4.87 °C by year 20. Is it about to stop, or will it keep going? That is the week's question: how warm, and when.

    Energy in, energy out. The Earth is warmed by sunlight. It reflects some of it, and sends the rest back to space as infrared heat. When the two balance, the temperature stays where it is.

    The Earth's energy flows today, in watts per square metre averaged over the globe. At the top: 240 of sunlight absorbed (yellow) and 239 of infrared heat leaving for space (red). The 1 left over, "net absorbed" at the bottom left, is today's energy imbalance: the Earth is gaining heat. The greenhouse effect is the 159 that the surface emits but the atmosphere holds back. Diagram: R. Wentworth, from IPCC AR6 numbers (CC BY-SA 4.0), via Wikimedia Commons.

    Energy imbalance. N: the sunlight the Earth absorbs minus the heat it sends back to space, in watts per square metre; while N is above zero the Earth keeps gaining heat and keeps warming. Think of a bathtub with the tap on and the plug out: the water rises until it drains as fast as it pours in. CO2 holds back some outgoing heat, so when it jumps, the imbalance jumps.     The imbalance shrinks as the Earth warms. It starts at 7.69 W m⁻² and is down to 2.9 W m⁻² by year 20: a warmer Earth sends more heat to space, as a fuller bathtub drains faster. The warming stops when the imbalance reaches zero.

    In this notebook $T$ always means the surface warming in °C, and $N$ the imbalance. The simplest picture (Gregory and others, 2004) says $N$ falls in a straight line as $T$ grows:

    $$N = F - \lambda\,T$$

    • Radiative forcing. F: the energy imbalance a change such as more CO2 causes before the Earth has warmed at all, in watts per square metre. It is where the line starts, at $T = 0$;
    • Climate feedback parameter. λ: how many extra watts per square metre a warmer Earth sends back to space for each degree it has warmed. (λ, lambda.) It is minus the slope of the line.

    If the line holds all the way, the warming stops where $N = 0$, that is at $T = F / \lambda$.

    Think. In year 20 the warming is 4.87 °C, well short of 7.43 °C. What holds the Earth back from warming faster?

    The kept energy has to warm something. Heat capacity. C: how much energy, kept for a year, it takes to warm a part of the Earth by one degree; a big C warms slowly. On the same stove a small pot boils in minutes and a big one takes much longer. The surface and the upper ocean warm together, like one pot, or one box:

    How fast the box warms is the slope of its warming curve, written $dT/dt$: the rise in $T$ (°C) over a short run of time (years), as a line's slope is its rise over its run. The box:

    $$C\,\frac{dT}{dt} = F - \lambda\,T$$

    $C$ is in W m⁻² years per °C. At first $T$ is 0, all of $F$ warms the box, and it warms fast; as $T$ grows, $F - \lambda T$ shrinks and the warming slows.

    Stepping the equation. Divide both sides by $C$: the slope is

    $$\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{F - \lambda\,T}{C}.$$

    Take a run of time $\Delta t$ (say "delta t", a step of time). Over it the slope is about rise over run, the change in $T$ over the step:

    $$\frac{T_{\text{next}} - T}{\Delta t} \approx \frac{F - \lambda\,T}{C} \qquad\text{so}\qquad T_{\text{next}} = T + \frac{F - \lambda\,T}{C}\,\Delta t.$$

    Start from $T = 0$ and apply it again and again: each step adds the slope times the step. With $\Delta t$ = 1 year, the step is a year:

    $$T_{\text{next year}} = T + \frac{F - \lambda\,T}{C}.$$

    A year is a short run for a curve this slow, so yearly steps are close enough for these pictures.

    The upper box loses heat to space, $\lambda T$, and passes heat down, $\gamma\,(T - D)$, more when the gap between the boxes is larger. The deep box keeps what it gets:

    $$C\,\frac{dT}{dt} = F - \lambda\,T - \gamma\,(T - D) \qquad C_{\text{deep}}\,\frac{dD}{dt} = \gamma\,(T - D)$$

    • $D$: the deep ocean's warming, in °C; the table has no column for it;
    • $\gamma$ (gamma): how many W m⁻² pass down for each degree the upper box is warmer;
    • $C_{\text{deep}}$: the deep ocean's heat capacity, much bigger than $C$.

    2. Can a network predict the next 130 years from the first 20?¶

    A neural network draws curves, not only lines. It is built from small pieces called neurons, loosely copied from nerve cells.

    Top, a nerve cell: signals arrive at its dendrites, the cell body (soma) adds them up, and the long axon passes a signal on to other cells. Bottom, the artificial neuron: each input $x$ times a weight $w$, the products added up with a bias $b$, then an activation $\varphi$, an S-curve, decides what to pass on. Figure via the Kardashev Scale wiki.

    Neuron. A weighted sum of its inputs plus a constant, then an activation: the piece a network is built out of. With inputs $x_1, x_2, \ldots$ its weighted sum is $z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \ldots + b$, where $b$, the bias, is a constant. On its own that is a straight line.

    Activation. What a neuron does to its weighted sum before passing it on; tanh turns it into a smooth step between −1 and 1. Without it, a stack of straight lines is still one straight line. Ours is $\tanh(z)$: a smooth S-curve from −1 to 1, like week 9's S-curve stretched to run from −1 to 1.

    Neural network. Layers of neurons, each layer feeding the next; adding up many smooth steps lets the whole draw curves. Neurons side by side make a layer; each layer's outputs are the next layer's inputs. Ours reads the year and gives the warming, through two layers of 32 neurons.

    A network built up a layer at a time: one neuron, then a layer of them, then our network of two layers. Each line is a weight; each neuron also has a bias. Training changes all of them. Drawn for this course.

    A network chooses its own weights by making a loss small.

    Loss. The one number the network is trying to make small. Ours is the mean squared miss, the one that fits a straight line.

    Gradient descent. Nudge every weight a little in the direction that makes the loss smaller, and repeat. Like walking down a hill in fog: you cannot see the bottom, but you feel which way the ground slopes, and you step downhill. Each step moves a weight $w$ against the slope of the loss, its gradient:

    $$w_{\text{new}} = w - \eta\,\frac{d\,\text{loss}}{dw}$$

    Learning rate. How big each nudge of gradient descent is: too small and it crawls, too large and it overshoots. Here $\eta$ (eta) = 0.1.

    See it. With many weights the loss is a landscape, and gradient descent walks down it:

    A loss landscape: the height is the loss, the two directions across are two weights, and the arrow is gradient descent walking downhill to a low point. Welch Labs animates it in The most cited paper of the century is a brilliant hack, from 13:20.

    A simple case with a known answer: the loss $(w - 3)^2$ is smallest at $w = 3$. Its gradient is $2\,(w - 3)$ (calculus: the slope of $x^2$ is $2x$): −6 at $w = 0$, so stepping against it moves $w$ up, toward 3.

    3. Can a network that obeys the energy balance do better?¶

    Physics-informed network. A network whose loss adds how badly its curve breaks a known equation, so it must fit the data and obey the physics at once. Like a student who must match the answers at the back of the book and also obey the rules of algebra: the rules guide them where the book gives no answer. Our rules are the two-box equations, which hold in every year, the 130 without data too.

    Its loss has two parts. The data loss compares the network with CESM2 in the 20 years we have; the physics loss checks the two-box equations in all 150 years. Drawn for this course.

    The same loop, one more loss. Your turn 6's loss was a data loss. Your turn 7 keeps it and adds a physics loss. Four things are new; the next cells take them one at a time.

    1. Two outputs, $\hat T$ and $\hat D$: the last layer is nn.Linear(32, 2).
    2. The data loss checks the imbalance too. The network's imbalance is $\hat N = F - \lambda \hat T$, and its miss against CESM2's $N$ is added to the warming's. Both are needed: from the warming alone, the network cannot tell $F$ from $\lambda$.
    3. The physics loss, from the equations' residuals (below).
    4. Five numbers learned with the weights, $F, \lambda, \gamma, C, C_{\text{deep}}$ (below).

    3. The physics loss. Equation residual. How far a curve is from obeying an equation: the left side minus the right side, zero when the equation holds. For each box, the left side of its equation minus the right side, in every year:

    $$r_{\text{surface}} = C\,\frac{d\hat T}{dt} - \left(F - \lambda \hat T - \gamma(\hat T - \hat D)\right), \qquad r_{\text{deep}} = C_{\text{deep}}\,\frac{d\hat D}{dt} - \gamma(\hat T - \hat D).$$

    Each is 0 in a year where the curves obey the equation. One more is the start, r_start: both warmings at year 0, when nothing had warmed yet, so it too should be 0. The physics loss is the mean of each residual squared, added up, and training pushes it toward 0.

    The slopes $d\hat T/dt$ and $d\hat D/dt$ come from PyTorch: torch.autograd.grad gives the slope of anything the network computed with respect to an input it tracks. This is why the network uses tanh: a tanh network draws a smooth curve, so its slope is smooth too; a ReLU network (one of the Playground's choices) draws straight pieces joined at corners, so its slope jumps.

    4. Five positive numbers. A heat capacity cannot be negative, nor can the others; learned directly, one turned negative. So the network learns their logarithms, log_numbers, and uses torch.exp(log_numbers), $e$ to the power of each: always positive, and $e^0 = 1$, so each starts at 1.     5. Where do physics-informed networks earn their place?¶

    Where a trusted equation is known but hard to step, or where the data are scattered. Our climate problem could be stepped a year at a time, and a fitting program could do the same job. Two Earth-science problems where an equation in the loss earns its keep:

    • How Antarctic ice shelves flow. Satellites see how fast an ice shelf moves and how thick it is, not how stiff the ice is. Wang, Lai and others (Science, 2025) trained networks to match the satellite data and obey the equations of ice flow, and learned the flow law and stiffness maps of whole shelves.
    • How long a seismic wave takes to travel. Locating an earthquake needs the travel time between any two points. EikoNet (Smith, Azizzadenesheli and Ross, 2021) trained a network on the travel-time equation for a known three-dimensional velocity model, with no travel times to learn from: the equation was the loss.

    In both, as in ours, the answer is only as good as the equation in the loss.

    Week 12 summary¶

    The science¶

    If CO2 jumped to four times, how warm would Earth get, and when?
    If CO2 jumped to four times, the CESM2 climate model warms 3.96 °C in 10 years, 4.87 °C in 20 and 7.55 °C in 150, and is still gaining 2.36 W m⁻²: fast for a few years, then slowly while the ocean takes up heat. Each degree of warming sends λ more heat to space, but CESM2's λ falls as it warms (1.14 in years 1-20, 0.41 after), so the first 20 years cannot say where the warming stops. This is one model's answer to a jump that will not happen, not a forecast for the real Earth. A network is trained by one loop: predict, measure the loss, take the gradient, step downhill. A physics-informed network is the same loop with a second loss: the data loss compares it with the data where there are data, and the physics loss, the squared residuals of the equations, holds it to the physics in every year, data or not. It learns the equations' numbers with its weights. From 20 years, a plain network goes wherever its weights give (8.24 °C at year 150, too warm) and the physics-informed one stays close (7.02 °C, against 7.55), but with the wrong imbalance (0.32 W m⁻² at year 150, against 2.36), because its equation holds λ fixed. A physics-informed network is only as right as its equation: a right answer does not prove the mechanism.

    • Climate model. A computer program that steps the physics of the air, the ocean, ice and land forward in time, on a grid around the globe; its output is a simulation, not a measurement.
    • Energy imbalance. N: the sunlight the Earth absorbs minus the heat it sends back to space, in watts per square metre; while N is above zero the Earth keeps gaining heat and keeps warming.
    • Radiative forcing. F: the energy imbalance a change such as more CO2 causes before the Earth has warmed at all, in watts per square metre.
    • Climate feedback parameter. λ: how many extra watts per square metre a warmer Earth sends back to space for each degree it has warmed.
    • Pattern effect. λ changing as the Earth warms, because the pattern of warming over the globe changes and some regions send heat to space more easily than others; in most climate models λ gets smaller with time.
    • Heat capacity. C: how much energy, kept for a year, it takes to warm a part of the Earth by one degree; a big C warms slowly.
    • Deep ocean. The cold water below a few hundred metres. It warms slowly, by taking heat from the upper ocean, and while it does the surface warms more slowly too.
    • Neuron. A weighted sum of its inputs plus a constant, then an activation: the piece a network is built out of.
    • Activation. What a neuron does to its weighted sum before passing it on; tanh turns it into a smooth step between −1 and 1. Without it, a stack of straight lines is still one straight line.
    • Neural network. Layers of neurons, each layer feeding the next; adding up many smooth steps lets the whole draw curves.
    • Loss. The one number the network is trying to make small.
    • Gradient descent. Nudge every weight a little in the direction that makes the loss smaller, and repeat.
    • Learning rate. How big each nudge of gradient descent is: too small and it crawls, too large and it overshoots.
    • Epoch. One pass through all of the training data.
    • Physics-informed network. A network whose loss adds how badly its curve breaks a known equation, so it must fit the data and obey the physics at once.
    • Equation residual. How far a curve is from obeying an equation: the left side minus the right side, zero when the equation holds.
